# Engagement geometry: full angular coverage and force paths

**The leading principle demonstrator is one axially sliding, screw-keyed carrier with an axial permanent magnet and one continuous 35° half-angle conical friction interface.** The stationary air coil below it provides lift or downward release force. There is no separate synchronization stage. This is selected for phase independence and inspectable force paths, not demonstrated torque or dense-array superiority. The physical delivery tests magnetic force first; clutch machining/printing waits for measured force.

## Serious alternatives

| Geometry | Every initial relative angle, with input initially stationary | Torque, stability and release | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight-sided face dogs with flat tops | Seated angular windows engage directly. Overlapping tops support axial load without positive flank overlap. No tangential gradient on a flat contact plateau. | Full straight flanks have no ideal axial separating force. At torque T, axial sliding friction can be ~μT/r. Bidirectional; no inherent overload slip. | Efficient ON state; conditional seating. Do not call all dog couplings impossible because this profile has blocking intervals. |
| Rounded/tapered face dogs | A slope can rotate a free output into a recess. Rounded summits have zero slope at an exact peak and a finite low-torque neighborhood; friction and screw load widen it. | Available aligning torque is `−Fz dh/dθ`. A shallow face might remain on its ramp and cam out under load. Add straight flank below lead-in, count the extra travel. | Real self-alignment in part of the phase space; not a demonstrated all-angle loaded coupling. |
| Sharp point/recess, including asymmetric ramp | An ideal point can make a peak unstable, but at the exact symmetric summit no preferred tangential direction is established. An asymmetric smooth periodic envelope still has extrema. A cusp may be destabilized by compliance, imperfections or external torque. | No finite static aligning-force margin at the ideal dead configuration. Real printed top flats and rounded roots change that neighborhood; tall sharp points reduce root strength. | E-298's specific plateau and broken parts inform print dimensions, not a prohibition on other point couplings. |
| Angularly compliant or radially floating positive member | Extra rotational/radial freedom can provide an escape path and bias even while the main shafts remain stationary. A biased pawl, flexible tooth or helical slide can create relative motion internally. | Requires counting the compliant member/link, bias, travel stop and fatigue; reversible torque may need opposed features. | Possible, not disproved. No simpler complete constrained bidirectional implementation established here. |
| Flat annular friction face | The contact set is unchanged for all θ in [0,360°); can close at rest without either shaft rotating. | `T=μ F r_eff`. Low actuator force means limited torque. Axial gap removes contact OFF; dirt/runout can restore drag. | Simplest geometric baseline, weaker torque per axial force than a cone. |
| Continuous cone, half-angle α | Same surface at every θ. Can close at rest; slight lateral seating may occur but angular indexing is unnecessary. | `N=F/sin α`, `T=μ F r_eff/sin α`; both torque signs. Small α boosts torque but risks wedging. Passive taper release requires roughly `tan α > μ_axial`. | **Selected α=35°**, not E-300's 45°. No stationary selector rubbing against the moving carrier. |
| Cone + dogs on the same carrier | Friction can transmit torque immediately at every phase. If that torque exceeds resistance, it can rotate the output into the dogs. At equal locked angular speed, there is no guaranteed remaining indexing motion. | One moving carrier is possible, but two contact-height conditions and positive seating/release still need tolerances. If load exceeds initial friction torque, it can remain blocked at a dog summit. | Useful hybrid in some loads, not an all-phase escape from the low-force friction budget. No extra prototype justified. |
| Wrap spring / ball ramp / self-energizing wedge | Can contact without tooth phase; relative tangential motion creates clamp amplification. | Self-locking/hysteresis, release under load and opposite direction require concrete design; often opposed mechanisms or a release cage. | Could improve force economy but adds parts and tolerance dependence. Not chosen merely to force success. |

No phase-search controller is prescribed. **Static self-alignment and dynamic capture are different questions.** If a shared input is already rotating, an unseated dog sees relative motion; a six-dog pattern offers an opening every 60°, with at most one pitch to a repeat opportunity. That movement arises from the shared drive, not an assumed static geometric solution. Impact, tooth bounce, partial-depth loading and output disturbance still need testing. Do not use a prescribed CAD rotation as proof that contact forces cause it.

## Numerical phase investigation and its limits

[Reproducible model](analysis/model.py), [full-period samples](analysis/results/phase-rounded.csv), and [plot](analysis/results/phase-envelopes.svg) evaluate 0–360° with 0.01° internal steps for six-period triangular, rounded and flat-topped contact envelopes. These are newly defined idealized geometries, **not** copied E-298 meshes. The continuous formulas include exact peaks and valleys, so conclusions do not depend on a coarse sample accidentally missing the dead phase.

For height 0.6 mm and six periods, the triangular slope magnitude is `nh/π=1.146 mm/rad`. At 30 mN axial load, maximum ideal aligning torque is **0.0344 N mm** before friction, below the E-303 light starting scenario 0.0597 N mm. A sinusoidal envelope has maximum slope `nh/2=1.8 mm/rad`, hence **0.054 N mm**, and zero at peaks and troughs. Its finite weak-angle neighborhoods grow as opposing torque rises. A 12%-period top flat has genuinely blocked intervals. Increasing force/height/teeth changes these values; it does not justify a universal impossibility claim. Frictionless extrema are not all mechanically stable: the peak is unstable if unconstrained, yet still has no finite deterministic starting margin under perfectly balanced loading.

For a cone, an analytical rotational-invariance argument covers every angle: its surface is `z(r)`, independent of θ. Contact and torque radius are unchanged under θ→θ+δ. The limiting uncertainties are runout, roughness, centering and friction, not tooth phase. At finite load, “contact at every phase” does **not** mean “sufficient torque at every phase”; a warped or eccentric actual print may give angle-dependent pressure. The conditional physical protocol samples 24 angles, both directions and repeated worst cases; sampling is evidence, not a mathematical proof of all manufactured conditions.

## Selected geometry and independent support

[Dimensioned section](images/concept-section.svg) · [Exploded view](images/concept-exploded.svg) · [Parametric envelope CAD](cad/concept.scad).

The deliberately enlarged principle concept has a **14×14×50.4-mm housing/actuator envelope**, Ø13-mm input gear envelope (24 teeth, module 0.5, 12-mm pitch diameter), cone radii **4–5 mm**, half-angle **35°**, and **0.60-mm axial stroke**. The gear tooth form and functional E-295 screw thread are marked envelopes in this concept CAD, not production meshes. They are not included in the force-test plate. The coil is the real 140-turn Ø4.8–7.32 ×3.6-mm winding from the force rig. Its magnet moves from lower-face coordinate **−1.0 mm OFF to −0.4 mm ON** relative to the winding's top. Positive z is upward. N is the upper magnet face; current creating upward B attracts it downward, opposite current lifts it toward the input cone.

- **Pin load:** terrain → nonrotating E-295 pin → thread → resident screw with integral thrust collar → one of two thrust washers → fixed frame. The upper thrust stack has 0.10-mm nominal endplay. Four integral corner webs connect the frame plates without blocking the side gear opening; the two square pin-guide lands are 10 mm apart. Two separate frame journal lands guide the screw; the pin's spaced square guides react torque and side load. The magnet and coil are absent from this terrain-load path.
- **Output coupling:** an integral double-D screw extension engages a matching sliding carrier bore. Across-flats 2.7 mm on nominal Ø3.2 shaft; concept bore clearance 0.10 mm per face, guide overlap ≥3 mm throughout stroke. This is a hypothesis needing physical tolerance trials, not a reuse of E-295's successful thread relief. The shaft ends above the magnet pocket floor; no hidden axial support through the magnet.
- **Selection:** the carrier and magnet alone translate 0.60 mm. Carrier mass is budgeted at 0.4 g including magnet, adhesive and capture film; weigh the eventual part. Coil reaction goes directly to its fixed bracket. Magnetic force presses the carrier cone upward into the input socket. No selector bearing rubs against a rotating flange.
- **Input support:** shared spur drive → local input gear/socket → conical friction contact → carrier → double-D → screw. The input has its own two radial lands and two thrust washers in the frame. The upper washer reacts the axial clutch load; the drive motor is not its thrust bearing. Gear side load also goes to this support. Housing includes an opening for the meshing shared gear; the common shaft/gear train remains shared infrastructure, not a demonstrated 6,400-way drivetrain.
- **OFF:** the input keeps spinning with a positive axial cone separation. The carrier remains keyed to the output and rests on a fixed lower stop (frame-integral annular ledge, top z2.8 mm, Ø3.8-mm central clearance); a backdriven screw can rub that stop. No zero-drag or passive terrain-height-retention claim follows. The OFF gap is 0.60 mm axially (~0.344 mm normal to the cone), before axial endplay, warp and debris. Allocate no more than 0.15 mm combined approach error for an initial trial; measure actual clearance.
- **Release:** reverse field drives the carrier down. Under load, sliding spline friction and any conical sticking oppose release. After power loss the air coil has no ferromagnetic latch: gravity supplies only ~3.9 mN. It can return an unloaded, freely sliding vertical carrier; **safe disengagement while torque remains is not guaranteed**. Cut shared rotary power on loss of selector power, allow inertia to decay, then verify drop. A spring, latch or stored-energy release would be additional counted hardware if required. None is hidden here.

There are **15 discrete cell-associated pieces in this explicit concept**: pin; integral screw/extension; sliding carrier; magnet; retaining film; input gear/socket; four thrust washers; two frame halves (shareable plate allocation); bobbin body; bobbin flange; wound coil. Two adhesive operations, coil terminations, shared frame fasteners, and electronics remain additional operations/hardware. Three rotary bodies (screw, carrier, input); one independently translating clutch body (carrier). Four thrust interfaces and radial/slide guide functions are real, even when molded into existing parts. This is not a two-part clutch merely because it has one magnet and one coil.

## Torque, losses, obstruction and release

For uniform cone pressure, `r_eff=(2/3)(ro³−ri³)/(ro²−ri²)=4.519 mm`. With guide friction during axial movement, a conservative available-torque model is

`T = k(Fmag−mg)/(1+k μslide/rspline)`, where `k=μcone r_eff/sin(35°)`.

The model uses μslide=0.15, rspline=1.5 mm and μcone=0.15/0.25/0.40 as **assumptions**. Screw guide/thrust losses already included in the E-303 scenarios are not added again. Input support drag is paid by the shared motor: at μthrust=0.1, F=0.05 N, radius 5.7 mm, it is about 0.029 N mm per active input, plus radial gear friction. Coaxial guide misalignment could consume the entire 4-mN gravity return margin; E-302 makes this a practical concern.

At μcone=0.25, the nominal force thresholds are **40.2 mN for the light 0.0597-N-mm scenario**, **212 mN for moderate 0.343 N mm**, and 1.34 N for the imperfect-guide case. The air model reaches the light scenario around **0.403 A /0.438 W**, without margin. At 0.5 A it predicts approximately **0.0756 N mm**; the moderate scenario would require an inadmissible ~2.13 A /12.24 W in this tiny winding. Those extrapolations reject that operating point, not authorize it. [Force requirements](analysis/results/required-force.csv).

A positive straight-flank dog could carry much larger seated torque at low axial load, but release friction alone at μ=0.15 and r=1.5 mm is ~6 mN for light and 34 mN for moderate torque, before guide/magnet losses. A 20° loaded ramp instead creates a separating force ~`T tan20°/r`: 14.5/83 mN. Stress at a dog root of 0.5 mm² carrying 0.343 N mm at r=2 mm is nominally 0.343 MPa if one tooth bears the full load; real layer adhesion, root concentration and impact dominate. This is a load scale, not a validated strength rating.

Conical slipping is bidirectional and can limit obstruction torque, but static/kinetic μ, temperature, surface wear and pressure change the threshold. At 600 rpm and 0.076 N mm, a slipping cell dissipates ~4.8 mW mechanically, while coil heating is much larger. The low friction torque still must lie above real running torque and below measured damage torque. Neither boundary has been measured. A blocked pin can remain selected and heat the coil even when the clutch slips; timeout and shared-drive stop are part of the bench protocol.

Manufacturing sensitivity: at α35°, 0.1-mm radial profile error changes seating height ~0.143 mm. Surface normal gap, cone roundness, spline looseness and independent input/output axes interact. No rubber lining is assumed: adding one changes thickness, μ, wear and cost. A very shallow cone, e.g. 10°, amplifies axial force ~5.76× but is self-lock prone once μaxial>0.176; 35° tolerates up to ~0.700 in the elementary taper criterion, still not a guarantee against edge binding or adhesion.

## Concrete positive-coupling reserve: potentially much lower holding power

A genuinely competitive alternative is **six straight dogs on one sliding carrier**, on radii1.5–2.3 mm, each20° wide,0.6-mm total height including a0.2-mm lead-in, with≥0.4-mm straight overlap and an~0.8-mm selector stroke. The other face has the complementary repeated dog pattern; baseline contact angles repeat every60°. This is a dimensioned analytical candidate, not a new manufactured test.

For ideal unchamfered20° dogs, relative phase modulo60° in(20°,40°) permits axial entry; the complementary40° interval initially overlaps tops. The exact boundary is a contact/tolerance case, not counted as a guaranteed clear window. Lead-ins provide torque gradients around some boundaries; increasing bevel width reduces straight-flank engagement or increases travel. At30 rpm input motion, each20° full-entry window lasts111 ms and a complete period333 ms, much longer than the few-ms ideal axial transit. That establishes a possible dynamic capture opportunity from every phase, **provided input and output actually have relative motion** and the carrier can slide.

If the output is held by load, top friction of roughlyμ F r=0.25×0.03×1.9≈0.014 N mm is below the light provisional screw starting torque; the shared input can slide over the tops and reach an entry window. If the output load is very small, top friction may entrain it at a blocked phase and it can rotate without full positive seating. That is temporary friction transmission, not proof of full engagement; a later load change can cause slip/impact. Exact tip contact, roughness and guide wedging can also delay drop. There is no prescribed phase-search algorithm in this analysis; relative motion in this case comes from the shared rotary input. Static self-alignment with both shafts initially stationary still obeys the ramp-force analysis above.

Once seated on straight flanks, torque does not ideally eject the carrier. A vertical0.4-g carrier needs only~3.9 mN to oppose gravity, about 0.039 A/~4.1 mW in the modeled coil, before vibration, friction margin and electrical losses. Engagement may use~0.3 A briefly. This is a substantial potential energy advantage over a continuously clamped cone. It is **not** a pulse-only bistable latch: power loss should allow drop only after torque/friction permits. Moderate-load release needs roughly34 mN spline/flank sliding force plus weight and imperfections;0.5-A magnetic release~50 mN may have insufficient margin. Straight dogs also have no inherent obstruction slip, so per-branch torque protection must be added or proven unnecessary for the tested load.

The six-dog geometry can be bidirectional, with approximately 20° nominal angular freedom between opposing20° teeth in a60° pitch; tolerated backlash is a system decision. Contact stress, layer strength and partial-depth impacts need measurement. Compared with the cone, this candidate offers potentially much more seated torque per watt, at the price of conditional dynamic seating, backlash, impact and loss of inherent overload slip. **It is not rejected as impossible.** The force screen is reusable for either surface. The cone is the current concrete concept because its static all-angle contact is established geometrically and it provides a simple slip experiment; no unmeasured friction coefficient or unperformed dog test has been promoted to physical evidence.

### OFF torque scale

In an isolated concentric air-core cone with a maintained gap, direct contact torque is absent geometrically. A parallel-disc air-shear estimate at 600 rpm, air viscosity1.8×10⁻⁵ Pa s,0.344-mm normal gap and 4–5-mm radii is only order0.000002 N mm; the real conical-flow result is of the same small scale, not a measured drag bound. A backdriving output can rub its lower carrier stop: μ0.1×3.9 mN×2 mm≈0.00078 N mm, plus resident screw bearings. Warped contact, dirt and magnetic/guide side loads can be much larger. Use a provisional OFF acceptance of<0.006 N mm (10% of light starting scenario), with an instrument resolving well below that. A torque reading below instrument resolution must be reported as an upper detection limit. This force rig does not measure OFF torque.
