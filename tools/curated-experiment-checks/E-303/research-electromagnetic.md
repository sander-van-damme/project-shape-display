# A, C and D — magnetic actuation and the missing mechanical functions

[Experiment](engineering-report.md) · [Calculations](calculations.md) · [Dimensioned A drawing](concepts/a-magnetic-concept.svg) · [Parametric concept](concepts/a-magnetic-concept.scad)

Engineering assessment, 23 October 2026. The CAD below is a **concept exposing component and tolerance requirements**, not a fabrication release or proof of magnetic force. No magnetic clutch has been physically tested for this project. E-295 provides the actual Ø3.2 mm, 3 mm lead, 90° thread and 4.4 mm pin; E-301 establishes qualitative motion, not a torque number. E-300 already identified the danger of stationary thrust friction in a pressed rotating assembly.

## A: conclusion before detail

A stationary coil can increase friction torque while the input rotates. A standard ferromagnetic washer can be the armature. However, the combination **rigid washer on a rigidly axially supported screw + independently axially supported input + no compliance** does not establish the desired force path: additional magnetic force can go straight into the screw thrust bearing instead of increasing face pressure. An always-contact interface needs controlled compliance, endplay or a floating member; eliminating a separate sleeve does not eliminate that mechanical function.

The proposed A reference integrates a compliant armature carrier into the rotating output body. That avoids a separately sliding sleeve but still has **one elastic selection degree of freedom**, two rotating assemblies, an armature washer, two magnetic poles, two windings and a return yoke. Low OFF drag demands extremely small neutral preload, runout and debris. These have not been demonstrated with FDM.

A useful first test is therefore the [unpowered normal-force friction screen](../../../owner-print-tests/2026-10-23-clutch-friction-screen/test.md). Measure actual friction and unavoidable tare before winding a coil. It does not validate a magnet, the compliant carrier or dense packaging.

## A1: complete proposed 9 mm contact / 12.4 mm housing reference

The actual purchased washer is **M3 DIN 9021 / ISO 7093, nominal 3.2 × 9 × 0.8 mm**, not an invented 10 mm metal disc. A 10 mm printed input ring contacts its outer annulus **r = 4.0–4.5 mm**, giving uniform-pressure effective radius 4.255 mm. The physical test also screens 4.7 and 10 mm faces independently; those are deliberately not a qualification of this magnetic assembly.

The casing, radial support, winding clearance and input thrust flange bring the reference to **12.4 mm overall**, although its friction diameter is only 9 mm. It does not fit one-, two- or four-height packing on a 5.08 mm square grid: nearest same-height distances are respectively 5.08, 7.184 and 10.16 mm before clearance. With a 0.30 mm pair clearance allowance, even four heights allow only 9.86 mm. More heights also require shafts and wiring to pass intervening cells; a circle-packing result alone does not resolve those obstructions.

### Three different force paths

1. **Terrain:** pin → thread → output screw → collar → stationary lower/upper thrust land → frame. The antirotation guide reacts screw torque. The moving input ring is not the terrain support.
2. **Clutch normal force:** stationary magnetic poles pull armature down → washer presses rotating input annulus → input thrust flange → stationary input support → frame → lower yoke/poles. The compliant armature carrier transfers torque to the screw without routing all attraction through its collar.
3. **Torque:** shared drive → input ring → washer friction → bonded carrier tabs → flexural spokes → output hub/screw → pin, with guide reaction to frame.

At contact, `N ≈ Fmag + armature weight + signed elastic preload`; for a carrier whose free position is above contact, subtract the elastic closure force. A fixed shaft is compatible with increasing N only because the carrier supplies compliance. A crude four-cantilever estimate with E = 2 GPa gives `kz ≈ 1.06 N/mm` for the stated beam dimensions. A **0.05 mm stack error therefore changes force by about 0.053 N**, comparable to the entire calculated magnetic force at modest current. Real root constraints, washer bonding and creep change this estimate. It is an assembly sensitivity, not a predicted calibrated spring.

Under an axial-load reversal, collar endplay shifts the carrier's neutral position: 0.02 mm would already alter preload by roughly 0.021 N in that estimate. This is why independent axial support alone does not eliminate load-dependent clutch drag. A small shimmed principle rig can expose the problem; tolerances at 6,400 cells remain unresolved. A steel washer 0.8 mm thick is not itself a low-force return flexure.

Input thrust drag belongs in **shared-drive torque and heat**, not a subtraction from friction torque delivered to the output when input speed is maintained. Output screw-thrust drag does subtract from useful screw torque. For example, a hypothetical input thrust coefficient 0.10 and mean radius 5.35 mm gives `Tinput-bearing ≈ 0.535 Nmm/N × N`; neither coefficient nor actual pressure distribution is known.

### Flux path, and why this is a clutch rather than a brake

The closed intended loop is left screw → left 0.10 mm working gap → rotating steel armature around its central hole → right 0.10 mm gap → right screw → lower steel washer yoke around its hole → left screw. The two coil polarities must aid round that loop. The printed rotating annulus occupies r4–4.5 mm, outside the poles, and contains no magnetic contact to a stationary part. All intended stator/armature magnetic boundaries are air gaps. **If a pole touches the armature, the assembly becomes a rubbing magnetic brake and the test is invalid.** Even with positive gaps, this non-axisymmetric two-pole field cycles through the rotating washer material and can create hysteresis/eddy-current braking and torque ripple. Those losses are unmeasured and are not included in the gross friction-torque estimate; the plastic input avoids a second ferromagnetic input rotor, not all magnetic loss. Neighbor leakage can also magnetize nominally OFF cells in a dense array. The useful force measurement must therefore be followed by torque/drag measurements with both polarity directions and neighboring energized poles before any array claim.

Both pole gaps are in series. The model conservatively uses 1.7 mm² effective screw-root area; the two parallel washer bridges have a gross combined section about `2 × 0.8 × (4.5−1.6) = 4.64 mm²`, but flux crowding at heads and pole tips is ignored. Plating, screw metallurgy, rough contacts and leakage can dominate. Ordinary low-carbon steel hardware is suitable for a test, but catalogue dimensions are not B–H data. The specifically sourced M2×8 socket-head shape uses hardened grade12.9 steel; its permeability/remanence may differ significantly from the grade4.6 pan-screw material alternative. Neither has a qualified magnetic curve here. Austenitic stainless steel is not a substitute.

The [reproducible model](calculations.md) assumes an effective 25 mm steel path, relative permeability 300 and 50 μm extra equivalent contact gap. Its 160 total turns / about 3.42 Ω calculation gives, at 0.30 A, approximately **0.181 T, 0.0443 N total attraction, 0.0566 Nmm gross friction torque at μ=0.30, and 0.308 W copper heat**. At 0.40 A: about **0.1005 Nmm and 0.547 W**. These are conditional magnetic-circuit estimates before carrier closure force, leakage and measured friction, not a promised coil rating. At 10 mW the same hypothetical configuration produces only about 0.00184 Nmm. The washer/coil concept does not establish that useful torque is available at 10 mW.

## A2: contact, OFF drag and thermal risks

Start with smooth steel against bare printed PLA because it is cheap and easily compared. Then try a thin PET face bonded to the input coupon. Neither material has a verified friction coefficient in this printed geometry. PLA ridges/dish produce intermittent contact; sanding would change the tested process and must be recorded. PET is readily replaceable but may lower friction; adhesive thickness, seams and wrinkles matter. Thin TPU can raise grip but adds creep, tack and OFF drag; it is a later coupon only if the first comparison warrants it. Cork/rubber/friction-lining products are generally too thick and variable at a 4–5 mm face.

Always touching means `Toff ≠ 0` in general: weight, preload, bearing drag, remanence, surface adhesion, runout and rubbing debris remain. Coercivity of an ordinary plated screw/washer is unspecified. Demagnetizing or reversing current may reduce remanence, but it adds control and does not remove contact friction. Wear may polish and reduce friction, or generate particles, burrs, film damage and increased drag. Neither trend may be assumed. Measure both directions before/after a finite slip cycle count and after power removal.

At 100 rpm, 0.10 Nmm of continuous slip dissipates 1.05 mW at that interface; 6,400 such slipping interfaces dissipate 6.70 W mechanically, separately from coil and bearing heat. A blocked pin is not automatically safe: screw force depends on torque, thread/guide friction and efficiency. A friction window is useful only when measured operating torque is reliably below slip torque and the resulting obstruction force is acceptable. The first experiment uses weights/force gauges, never a finger as the obstruction.

## A3: wound wire versus a PCB spiral

A PCB spiral can remain stationary, but it does not solve the magnetic return circuit. As an illustrative manufacturable layout, a 4.6 mm outer / 2.4 mm inner single-layer spiral with 0.10 mm trace and 0.10 mm spacing fits only about five turns radially. With 35 μm copper and about 55 mm path, its DC resistance is about 0.27 Ω. At 100 mA it supplies about **0.5 ampere-turn**, versus 16 ampere-turn for 160 wire turns at that current; equal MMF would demand ampere-level current in a fine trace. More layers add vias, board cost and thermal constraints. These are layout calculations, not a built PCB coil. Wound copper is the credible first actuator candidate; a bare coil beneath two discs without a return yoke is not an equivalent design.

## Repeated component count — do not count assemblies as single parts

The A concept has 2 baseline moving parts (pin, screw/output body, with integral compliant carrier), plus 1 rotating printed input, 1 armature washer, 2 stationary pole screws, 1 stationary yoke washer, 2 wound coils, 2 insulating bobbins and 2 split printed support/keeper pieces: **13 physical pieces including baseline, 11 incremental**, before adhesive, connection wire, selective shims and shared-frame fasteners. The output body's carrier is counted as a functional elastic moving element even if printed integrally. Four spokes do not become four independent moving parts. Continuous rotating assemblies: 2; independently translating terrain pin: 1; elastic selection motion: 1. If the carrier must be printed separately, add one component and its torque joint. Electronics are additional and itemized in sourcing/addressing documents. Supports could eventually be shared across a module, but their material, assembly and constraints remain.

For a single-cell assembly, add one selected PET thrust shim and two counted adhesive bonds (washer-to-carrier and fixed-support halves); this is **14 discrete pieces plus bonding material**, including baseline pin/screw. The bonded housing is a concept compromise for component count: servicing requires breaking that bond and replacing/rebonding a housing half. A reusable bolted housing adds its actual fasteners and envelope and is not silently included. The SCAD models the mating support envelope, not a qualified snap-fit. Standard purchased: four steel pieces and copper wire. Printable: output adaptation/carrier, input, supports, bobbins. Unusual processing: small coil winding, thin flexure printing, controlled bonding and shimming. **No custom machined metal** is specified. This is still considerably more assembly than “one washer and one coil”.

## C: bistable permanent-magnet clutch — preserve as fallback

A permanent magnet can retain a mechanism without continuous coil power, but an unpolarized soft-iron armature is attracted for either current polarity; reversing a bare coil is not a bidirectional actuator. A genuinely springless version needs a polarized moving magnet and a field geometry that creates opposite forces for the two pulses, with two demonstrable energy minima/stops. Commercial terminology is not enough: Kendrion's BI linear family explicitly uses a return spring; Shindengen distinguishes one-direction holding from two-direction holding with two coils/pole pieces. Their performance does not demonstrate the requested single-coil, springless 5 mm device. [Kendrion BI](https://www.kendrion.com/en/products-services/solenoids-actuators/linear-solenoids/bistable-solenoids), [Shindengen technical guide](https://smt.shindengen.co.jp/english/pdf/self_holding.pdf).

A concrete ring-magnet example is [EarthMag SKU2435, N45 Ø4 / ID2 / height2 mm](https://www.magnethandel.de/neodymium-ringmagnets-4-2-2-mm): supplier price €0.24 single or €0.19 at500, including German VAT, with stock/arrival not confirmed. Its ±0.1 mm tolerance and 2 mm bore do not fit a 3.2 mm screw or a robust sleeve around it: a reduced journal below the screw and its sleeve need their own space. The advertised holding force is a direct-contact pull test on thick steel, not force across our gaps. A reasonable **unverified fallback layout** is a printed sleeve continuously keyed to the input, carrying an axially magnetized ring magnet around the central shaft. It travels about 0.4–0.6 mm between a released stop and a shallow dog/friction face. Two stationary annular steel pole/stop regions retain noncontact magnetic gaps; **mechanical end stops belong to the rotating input hub**, so the latched sleeve is not pressed against a stationary brake. A stationary coil biases the two magnetic paths; merely drawing a coil concentrically around a symmetric magnet does not establish the correct switching-force sign. Pole geometry, switching margin and magnet grade require a force-vs-position measurement before CAD elaboration. An off-axis disc magnet fixed to a rotating sleeve rotates away from its stationary actuator: remedying that requires either an axisymmetric circuit or a separate stationary shuttle/thrust link, which adds parts.

Count baseline pin/screw 2; input rotor 1; sliding sleeve 1; ring magnet 1; stationary magnetic end washers 2; return yoke/core arrangement at least 1; coil 1; bobbin 1; split support/keeper 2: **at least 12 physical pieces**, plus retention/bonding, shims, leads and electronics. One additional sliding rotating member is unavoidable in this version. Required magnetic support may exceed that lower bound. A dog face gives low OFF drag and strong ON transmission but **does not slip on obstruction**. A friction face can slip, but permanent-magnet holding force must maintain useful pressure without dragging in OFF. Do not quietly give the dog version the user's desired slip behavior.

Holding coil power ideally tends to zero; driver sleep/leakage and shared-drive friction do not. Example pulse energy `I²Rt` for 0.5 A, 4 Ω, 20 ms is 20 mJ, explicitly an illustration with no verified switching force. 6,400 simultaneous pulses are 128 J and 3,200 A at the coils; staged selection is needed even when average energy is modest. A bipolar H-bridge or equivalent is more electronics than A's single low-side switch. Recommendation: **defer as principal mechanical fallback**, particularly if continuous coil heating kills A, until actual screw torque and A OFF drag are measured. No credible standard-part, single-coil 5.08 mm package has been established.

## D: conventional electromagnetic clutch — reference, not free simplification

Commercial friction clutches illustrate the functions A is attempting to simplify: a stationary field, rotating rotor, armature that can move axially, and a flexible torque link/return element. The return element creates separation after current removal and accommodates face wear/runout while retaining torque transmission. It is not merely an arbitrary spring. Miki's catalogue describes its stator/rotor magnetic gaps, armature plate spring and alignment requirements; its published construction is useful evidence of functions, not a source of pitch-compatible parts. [Miki manufacturer description](https://www.mikipulley-us.com/electromagnetic-e.m.-clutches), [manufacturer catalogue](https://www.mikipulley-us.com/uploads/CBcatalogue.pdf).

A printed diaphragm can combine torque transmission and return in one manufactured piece, but still adds compliance and fatigue/creep behavior. Gravity return would depend on orientation and acceleration and cannot guarantee release in both rotational directions. Positive mechanical retraction can eliminate a spring only by providing another motion source/linkage. Electromagnetic dogs need phase alignment, axial stroke and a release path under torque; they do not protect an obstructed pin through controlled slip.

Typical functional lower bound, counting the common pin/screw: pin, screw, input rotor, movable armature, flexible return or separate sleeve/return, coil, bobbin, stator pole/yoke and split support = **about 10–14 physical pieces**, depending on integration. At least one axial moving/flexing engagement member beyond the two rotating assemblies. No cost/torque figure for a nonexistent 5 mm purchased clutch is asserted. Recommendation: **defer as a reference/fallback**; if A cannot release reliably, accept that deliberate separation has a real mechanical cost rather than claiming it can simply be deleted.

## Finite decision and stop

The only prepared physical test is the unpowered friction screen. If useful ON torque demands normal force that would require unacceptable per-pin heat, or if the observed OFF drag approaches screw breakaway torque, stop A before adding electronics. If A remains plausible, an independently measured magnetic-force test using standard steel hardware is the next owner decision, not an automatic assignment. DES-009 remains primary throughout.
