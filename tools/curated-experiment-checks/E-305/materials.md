# Magnetic materials and remagnetization

E-305 · 25 October 2026 · Research and calculations; no new physical measurements.

**Buy a pre-magnetized axial Ø3 × 2 mm NdFeB magnet for the force experiment.** Ordinary steel can retain magnetization, and normal actuator fields can write some steels. That does not make a short steel button equivalent to NdFeB under a repeatedly reversed actuator field. Material, heat treatment, shape and the complete hysteresis history determine the result.

For the chosen disc, `B_axis(g)=Br/2[(g+t)/sqrt((g+t)^2+R²)−g/sqrt(g²+R²)]`. [Generated field table](analysis/results/magnet-field.csv) is free-space induction along its axis, not pull force or a measured surface field. Force is a field-gradient interaction and depends on the other body.

## Rotation, retention and tolerances

An axial disc/ring/cylinder retains the same magnetization vector when it spins about its axis. In an axisymmetric coil its ideal axial force is independent of screw angle and its magnetic torque about that axis is zero. A diametric magnet in a transverse stationary field experiences angle-dependent torque and force; reversing current can rotate it toward a new direction rather than translate it. A diametric magnet in an ideal axial field has no useful first-order axial `m·grad(B)` force, but can try to tilt. Radial/multipole rings could work with a matching structure, at increased pole/assembly complexity.

A solid disc below the output shaft avoids buying a ring and preserves magnetic volume. Glue onto a nonmagnetic carrier, never drill or tap a sintered magnet. The concept includes a thin retaining film wrap as a counted item; the bench uses a small epoxy bond plus polyimide tape around magnet/post, leaving the pole face clear. Verify bond before energizing. Keep magnet axes consistent with a reference magnet/compass; magnetic north is not identified by appearance. No hot soldering on the magnet. Nickel coating is not a structural shaft joint and may delaminate. Actual magnet maximum Ø3.1 ×2.1 mm and bond thickness belong in the gap measurement. Do not infer an FDM hole tolerance from a magnet's ±0.1-mm specification.

## What remagnetization can and cannot do

`B=mu0(H+M)`. Remanence is the residual induction/polarization on a specified hysteresis trajectory after reducing the *internal* applied field to zero. Normal coercivity Hcb is the reverse H at B=0; intrinsic Hcj concerns J=mu0 M=0. Neither number alone is the field required to fully magnetize an initially unmagnetized sample.

**Initial magnetization:** domain walls move and domains rotate on a virgin curve. A field sufficient to cross local barriers can leave a remanent state. **Maintenance:** a well-chosen permanent magnet needs no periodic supply; modest fields trace a recoil/minor loop. **Reversal:** an opposing field can irreversibly reduce or reverse M. Repeating a command that crosses the relevant switching distribution is not merely “refreshing” the magnet.

The open component's internal field is approximately `H_int=H_applied−N_d M`. A short Ø3 ×2 mm part has substantial self-demagnetizing field. Taking an illustrative `N_d=0.4`, an attempted 1 T polarization means about **318 kA/m** demagnetizing field. Compare 0.7 kA/m for the cited 1020 or 3.1 kA/m for oil-quenched C45. A rough knee scale `mu0 Hc/N_d` is only 0.0023/0.0098 T respectively. This is a shape/load-line screening estimate, not a prediction of the exact multidomain retained moment. It explains why large remanence measured on a closed specimen does not promise a strong open steel button. An elongated rod, keeper or closed return can improve the operating point, but changes this assembly.

The chosen air coil gives order **10 kA/m**, depending on position and current, at the proposed ≤0.5 A. That can magnetize or rewrite ordinary steel and overlaps the measured C45 writing fields. A steel-core actuator's locally concentrated fields can be larger but are limited by saturation, leakage and geometry. It cannot be represented by multiplying the air-field formula by an arbitrary relative permeability. In a soft induced armature, M follows H: attraction toward stronger field occurs for either polarity. A previously magnetized steel part might briefly repel before its magnetization changes; its full minor loops and shape decide that. Therefore software remagnetization is technically possible for a *specified suitable steel geometry*, but is an unreliable assumed substitute for the stable N48 dipole needed here.

Published approximate fields for ≥98% magnetization are **239–557 kA/m AlNiCo, 796–955 kA/m hard ferrite, and roughly 2,390–3,980 kA/m sintered NdFeB**, with grade/fixture dependence. The normal actuation winding is far below these. [Arnold magnetizing guidance](https://www.arnoldmagnetics.com/resources/magnetizing/). No magnetizing machine is designed or proposed.

Time matters until coil current, flux penetration and domain switching settle; magnetic viscosity and eddy currents can affect a pulse. More time at an inadequate field does not substitute for adequate peak H or transform soft iron into a high-coercivity material. Keeping this coil powered for minutes primarily adds heat. Thermal/chemical processing that changes steel coercivity is a separate manufacturing operation, not software magnetization. Do not confuse initial saturation, a small corrective pulse on a recoil curve, and full reversal.

Repeated reverse actuation of the selected N48 disc is unlikely to reverse it with this air coil: applied H is far below its room-temperature Hcj. That is not a universal demagnetization guarantee; self-demagnetization, temperature, edge fields and the actual demagnetization knee matter. Compare force before/after a defined reversal series, stay below 45°C at the fixture, and use a higher-coercivity grade if later data warrants it. Ordinary steel and AlNiCo need a much more cautious reverse-field analysis. Buying already magnetized discs avoids heat treatment, magnetization equipment, output sorting and a recurring remagnetization dependency for approximately €0.17 each at the documented tier.
