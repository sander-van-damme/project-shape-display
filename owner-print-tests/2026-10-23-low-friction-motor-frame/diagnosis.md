# Why the tested v3 binds as the pin rises

23 October 2026 · One motor / one pin · Baseline repository `c06a663`

**The most likely problem is alignment-dependent binding involving the long split square guide, the screw/pin pair and the rotor axis. The particular rubbing surface is not yet established.** The owner's latest clarification matters: the screw was the **long version**, print artifacts were removed, and the pin rose about **15 mm**. A later knife modification to the rotor base followed the bad tests; it cannot explain the original onset. The correct next step is one measured-travel, existing-parts isolation test, before spending a frame reprint on the wrong interface.

## Evidence and its limits

**Owner-reported physical evidence, received 23 October:** v3 was assembled and tested; the motor fits nicely; lower pin positions move relatively readily; greater extension causes increasing resistance and occasional stalls/missed motion in both directions; relieving enclosure pressure permits movement again. The pin was approximately 40 or 50 mm long, rose about 15 mm, and used the long screw. The owner reports removing all print artifacts; this is understood to include the wide print foot, whose absence should be checked during disassembly. After unsuccessful tests, the owner also shaved material with a knife from the screw/rotor base at the motor connection. Exact removed surfaces and amount are unknown. Keep before/after-shaving observations separate.

These observations establish a mechanical contribution sensitive to enclosure constraint. They do **not** locate the contact, measure friction, establish that the shaft is unloaded, or confirm a particular digital revision. The unknown original height datum also prevents treating “15 mm” as an exact CAD coordinate.

Earlier evidence remains relevant: E-301 demonstrated up/down motion at approximately 0.80 V Vref with a taped coupling and poorly stabilized motor; its calculated approximately 55 mA RMS was not measured. A was too tight, two N couplings broke, and C fitted well. The horizontal supported long screw printed poorly and supports were hard to remove; the short screw was better. Retired rotating-square interference and ineffective motor springs are not proven failures of this round-rotor v3. Compact five-pair hand tests favored approximately 0.175 mm radial thread clearance over 0.15/0.125; separate wider-nut tests showed variation and good results around 0.20. These are different geometries, not one tolerance curve or a motorized qualification.

Current during the newer tests is uncertain: approximately 1.2 V Vref / requested IRUN 10 was not necessarily actual IRUN 10; the software could revert to 5. UART timeout is a separate control failure. The reported approximately 18 commanded steps per revolution is unverified and does not replace the candidate 20-full-step specification. No firmware, current increase or new electronics is part of this recommendation.

**Repository discrepancy:** v3 `test.md`, `verification.md`, E-302 and the motor document described physical testing as absent. That blanket statement is superseded by this owner report. Their historical digital checks remain evidence about their particular files. We have not identified the owner's printed 3MF hash, and the shaved assembly no longer matches an untouched STL.

The C “prongs” are integral inward lobes connected to the thick outer rotor wall. The negative cavity leaves spaces between gear-engagement ribs; no radial slots cut through the outer wall and no designed flexible necks exist. **They are not three independent spring brackets.** Actual polymer deformation may occur, but angular compliance has not been designed or measured. Retain the successful radial profile; a more rigid or tighter coupling could increase unwanted side load.

## Axial and lateral support

Lifting a downward-loaded pin makes the pin react downward on the screw: pin thread → rotor bottom → washer → deck → frame. During lowering under that same downward load, the axial force can still follow this lower path; direction of rotation alone does not reverse thrust. Driving a pin downward against an upward resistance can instead pull the rotor upward: thread → rotor shoulder → upper stop → frame. At reversal the rotor can take up0.30 mm float, contributing to motion lost before the pin responds.

The upper stop is a genuine rotating thrust contact. A tilted rotor may touch it on one side while also rubbing the journal or washer, creating more drag than nominal clearance suggests. Flat clean seats matter. The single short journal and flat washer are not a kinematic guarantee against shaft side loads: the motor coupling is another alignment constraint. The pin's screw thread must not be substituted for a radial bearing; it carries load through helicoidal flanks, has fit clearance and can turn misalignment into friction.

The nominal journal's angular envelope is large:0.40/3.7≈0.108 rad before opposite-edge contact, an order-of-magnitude geometric bound rather than permissible operating tilt. The square guide's full-length envelope is approximately 0.40/40≈0.010 rad (0.57°); the long straight bore's corresponding small-angle envelope is approximately 0.32/39≈0.0082 rad (0.47°). Offsets consume these budgets. These calculations explain competing alignment constraints; they do not estimate actual friction or bending stiffness. Removing radial support would shift unwanted loads toward the coupling and motor.

## Travel: what 15 mm changes

For the modeled low position, pin bottom is27.9; at rise15 it is42.9 (plus any separately counted rotor float). The long screw still overlaps the complete lower6-mm threaded region. A short screw would have full6-mm overlap only through about 7 mm rise, partial overlap from 7 to13, and none at 15; **the owner has confirmed the long screw, so this is not their identified cause**.

Removing the print foot makes the long50-mm pair nominally collision-free at the audited15-mm positions. That is a diagnostic virtual removal of the foot, **not a proposed new printable part or authorization to trim a rotor**. It does not recreate the owner's knife-modified base. The actual40-mm pin, if that is what was used, is not supplied in this v3 pack and is not claimed to match the 50-mm mesh.

For an otherwise identical40-mm pin, calculated guide overlap would be35.2 mm at low and 29.8 at+15; tip overhang would be−4.8 to10.2 mm. For the 50-mm pin, overlap 40→29.8 and overhang5.2→20.2. Thus higher extension does not inherently increase friction: nominal overlap actually decreases at high extension. Under a lateral tip force, however, the larger lever arm and shorter support span can increase opposing guide reactions; bow, thread/runout and mismatched axes can then wedge. No lateral force, pin straightness or frame distortion has been measured.

The old3-mm limit comes from a bounded digital validation plus retained-foot packaging, **not** from 50-mm pin length or an intrinsic3-mm capability of the lead screw. Eliminating that foot alone does not qualify40-mm travel: thread overlap, antirotation support and lateral stability must all survive. We do not release a longer-stroke design here.

## Ranked diagnosis after the owner's clarifications

1. **Guide/closure alignment coupled to pin and screw straightness.** Best fit to both symptoms: the long split guide has no upper registration peg, only 0.20 per-face nominal clearance, and closing can constrain bowed/mismatched surfaces. At15 mm the pin has less supported length and greater overhang. Against a simple “too long guide” explanation: the nominal guide does not narrow with height, and reduced apparent contact area alone does not guarantee lower dry friction. The decisive check is the pin alone through the same physical height marks, closed versus relieved.
2. **Rotor/journal/thrust/coupling axes fighting each other under rising pin side load.** Opening can let the rotor or motor settle into a different alignment. A tight split journal, nonflat thrust seating or motor spacer bias can magnify this. Against a primary height-independent bearing explanation: the bearing geometry stays the same with rise, and the motor cavity fits well. Test rotor/washer without pin and motor only if guide and off-frame thread checks pass. The later knife shaving can change radial fit, thrust-face flatness or coupling seating; its effect is unknown and must not be backdated to the original failures.
3. **Position-dependent printed thread/straight-bore defects or screw runout.** The longer screw has negative prior print history; local support scars/seams can encounter the 6-mm internal thread at particular heights. The0.16 minimum straight-bore side gap also makes bow consequential. Against an isolated thread-only explanation: loosening an external frame should not fix a defect unless alignment was contributing. Test the same pair outside the frame at corresponding positions, without sanding first.

Overtravel beyond the old3-mm model is a confirmed *validation gap*, not proof of collision in the owner's modified long pair. Retained-foot collision and short-screw disengagement were important competing explanations and are demoted by the new owner information. Washer friction alone, motor torque inadequacy, or the mere appearance of C's gaps explain the height trend less well. Current uncertainty prevents a torque verdict; it does not erase the manual/closure evidence.

A nominal half pair contains about 10.51 cm³ of mesh material (~13.0g using1.24g/cm³ PLA), most of the previous full plate's filament. A new slice would be required for reliable time/mass; neither concept has one. This is why a generic replacement pair is not a small, decisive first test. A freestanding clearance coupon also fails to reproduce the full guide, closure and loaded axes.

**Exactly one selected action: the measured-travel isolation test in [test.md](test.md), on the existing assembly.** It preserves all support and successful interfaces, changes one constraint at a time, and can isolate guide, off-frame screw/pin, rotor support, or motor alignment without a new print. It cannot guarantee a unique rubbing surface if only the complete assembly binds; that outcome is explicitly retained rather than declaring a speculative fix. No printer-ready claim is made. Bambu Studio is not installed in this environment; this has no effect on the chosen zero-print test, and no unverified3MF is substituted.

## Verification and decision

`scripts/verify_geometry.py` reads actual baseline meshes and checks watertightness, physical rotations (determinant+1) for both handed halves, seated and lifted motor/rotor configurations, the 9.2-mm body and explicit appendage envelopes, nominal working poses, and retained-foot contact boundaries. It also evaluates a virtual footless long-pin assembly to separate the owner's altered travel from the original package. Exact checked poses, source hashes, overlaps and collision volumes are in [metadata/geometry-review.json](metadata/geometry-review.json). The motor's true tooth, glue, wire and tab shapes are not fully measured; allowance envelopes and intentional rib/gear-envelope intersection are identified, not mistaken for an exact gear mesh. No fresh slicer check is needed or claimed for unchanged archival parts.

Reproduce from this directory with Python packages `numpy`, `trimesh`, `manifold3d`: `python scripts/verify_geometry.py`. The repository baseline files are required. Source and generated evidence distinguish CAD from physical findings. The present audit does not model friction, print deformation, elastic clamping force, or the unknown knife-cut surfaces.

If the assembled mechanism passes the manual and short powered criteria in the protocol, it can be the basis of the next independently driven pin experiment **within that tested range**. If it binds, use the first failing isolation stage to name the implicated interface. If relief only works by allowing excessive motion, reject that condition. If driver faults obscure powered behavior, preserve manual findings and mark the powered result inconclusive. Stop here; no second print, electronics repair or motor-bank study is launched.
