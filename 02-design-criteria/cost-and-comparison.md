**Reference affordability benchmarks, not a fixed product ceiling:** purchased/nonprinted parts <$200 ideal, $200–400 attractive, ~$500 a useful original target. These figures encourage accessibility, but the actual D&D experience determines value. Sander could prefer a **really enjoyable ~$1,000 finished Shape Display** over a disappointing $500 version. That possibility is **not** a changed spending authorization, guaranteed user willingness to pay, or license to ignore costs. Compare low-cost and higher-value configurations and explain exactly what the extra price improves for play. Count motors, electronics, sensors, bearings, fasteners, wiring, guides, power supplies and all other bought parts.

**A research demonstrator and finished consumer value are separate decisions.** A working $1,000–$1,500 research prototype can be an excellent milestone. A higher-priced finished machine may also be worth investigating if it genuinely improves gameplay; it need not be forcibly cost-reduced to $500. Show a truthful ~$500 reference and the marginal improvement per extra cost. Neither a prototype envelope nor user willingness to consider a premium product authorizes a purchase; coordinate major expenditure with the owner/CEO. Do not conceal non-scalable repeated complexity behind vague future savings.

Printed parts have no formal cost ceiling. Still report material, print complexity, time, failures and assembly burden when comparing practical total cost. For outsourced precision prints, molds, casting, special materials, tools and service costs, report both purchased cost and all-in prototype/manufacturing economics while retaining the ~$500 benchmark comparison.

Compare: **enjoyment, immersion, legibility, miniature usability, game-flow and player waiting**, then resolution/pitch, board size, travel, neutral height, physical vs visual representation, surface gaps, bought and all-in cost, actuator/wiring/power scaling, **representative playable gameplay updates and separate dense stress cases**, regional isolation, reliability, durability, buildability and small-prototype testability. Distinguish assumptions, sourced facts, calculations, simulation, CAD, informal physical observations and documented measurements. Reliability and an executable computational verification path remain screening gates; eventual physical testability and qualification remain required.
