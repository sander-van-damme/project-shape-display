Final product goal — purchased/nonprinted components: <$200 ideal; $200–400 acceptable; $400–500 last resort; **target a maximum of $500**. Count motors, electronics, sensors, bearings, fasteners, wiring, guides, power supplies and all other bought hardware. Above $500 does not meet the eventual product cost goal.

**Research-stage feasibility is not an automatic NO-GO at $500.** A convincing working mechanism or integrated demonstrator costing around **$1,000–$1,500** in bought hardware could be a meaningful milestone: we presently lack a working product, and proving a robust, buildable mechanism may justify subsequent cost optimization. Those figures are illustrative research envelopes, **not a revised $1,500 final-product requirement or blanket purchase authorization**. Report its actual cost and a concrete, testable path to reduce it toward the $500 product goal. Do not prematurely reject otherwise promising, physically credible architectures solely for breaching the final price before that potential is assessed; do not bury fundamental non-scalable cost behind an assumed future optimization.

Printed parts have no formal cost ceiling. Still report material, print complexity, time, failures and assembly burden when comparing practical total cost. For outsourced precision prints, molds, casting, special materials, tools and service costs, report both purchased cost and all-in prototype/manufacturing economics without redefining the $500 product metric.

Distinguish assumptions, sourced facts, calculations, simulation, CAD, informal physical observations and documented measurements. Reliability and an executable computational verification path remain screening gates; eventual physical testability and qualification remain required.
