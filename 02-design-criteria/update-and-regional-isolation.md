**Stage distinction:** Complete-board latency, camera verification, homing/recovery and height drift metrics below guide **future integrated products**, not today's small basic up/down prototype. Temporary small drift is acceptable; mechanical neighbor interference is not. See [current priorities](current-prototype-priorities.md).

**Current player-wait benchmark, not an immutable product rejection gate: aim for a fully playable representative D&D update within about 30 seconds** on the 80×80 reference. Always report the actual complete duration against this reference. A different waiting time may be worth it when the real experience clearly improves; do not silently redefine success or omit preparation time. The clock starts when a new map/update is submitted and ends when the requested geometry is positioned, safely retained, sufficiently checked, settled and ready for miniatures. Include per-map digital preparation, physical pattern creation/rewrite, transport, indexing, reset/homing, actuation, height verification, recovery and settling; exclude genuinely one-time power-on setup. Buffering may improve visible latency but cannot erase continuing programming or sustained cycle time.

**Why the target exists:** a map change should not interrupt the fun of play for long. The current ~30-second threshold represents an acceptable player-facing wait, not a magic mechanical duration. Evaluate the experience of the Game Master and players, including setup, fog-of-war reveal, positioning miniatures, visual clarity, and recovery. The owner explicitly allows better UX alternatives that miss an initial timing number; evaluate the resulting play rhythm and get a deliberate product choice rather than hiding delays or declaring an alternative automatically unfit.

**First qualify the intended physical representation; then count pins, rows and seconds.** Classify each map feature by D&D function: physical ground/hills, walls and cover, cliffs/chasms, water, vegetation, small stones or props, texture and uncertain features. State whether physical elevation is *useful for play*, optional, or likely to make the map look worse. Compare alternative interpretations for ambiguous maps, including a flat projected/visual river versus raised banks, and actual cliff geometry versus a purely illustrated drop. Explain the selection to a player/owner before allowing it to determine an actuator winner. A river occupying many zero-height pixels may force elevated adjacent ground in one chosen encoding; do not mistake that for an intrinsic required raised-cell percentage.

For benchmark inputs, preserve semantic annotations, encoding policy, and at least two variants when the visual-vs-physical tradeoff can change architecture rankings. Include legibility from the table, miniature stability, gameplay movement/line of sight/cover, and Game Master reveal actions alongside pin occupancy and visited rows. Research published gameplay examples and player feedback when useful. If a consequential subjective decision cannot be resolved, ask Sander a specific question with concrete alternatives, mark provisional assumptions and keep unrelated work moving. Do not rewrite the original results of old experiments; identify their chosen rendering assumptions and rerun on newly justified input maps.

**Neutral/resting height is a design choice, not necessarily minimum extension.** Screen (A) minimum-rest/upward-only, (B) an elevated neutral/home plane permitting both downward and upward travel, and (C) map-dependent height baselines only if their preparation/re-zero cost is counted. E-254's Riverside Road heightfield has **1,951 water/base cells at 0 mm, 4,272 ground cells at +10 mm and 177 raised features at +20 mm**. Re-referencing ordinary ground as the neutral level yields **1,951 lowered cells (30.48%), 4,272 unchanged, 177 raised (2.77%)** while preserving those *relative heights*. This is a coordinate/workload illustration, not a proven physical machine or necessarily the best artistic rendering. A functioning design must provide signed travel, support, column length and clearance below the playing plane; the initial resting surface has to be established somehow. Initial flat-to-map changes, map-to-map transitions, visited rows and any collective reset remain real and must be counted. A smaller moved-cell fraction does not automatically mean proportionally faster actuation.

**Treat pitch as another gameplay-driven variable.** Compare the 5.08-mm/~6,400-cell reference with a quarter-square 6.35-mm (~4,000-cell) or other defensible grid, measuring whether walls, passages, miniature stability and relief remain useful before deciding how many mechanisms, head positions and actions are required.

**Do not equate a full-board map change with moving every pin (approximately 6,400 in the reference, possibly fewer in other designs).** Sander's gameplay expectation is that walls/barriers and other prominent terrain occupy only a fraction of a typical battle map, often leaving broad floor areas at a common baseline; tiny scenery details may be represented visually instead. This is a plausible use-case hypothesis, **not a measured raised-cell fraction, changed-cell fraction or fixed benchmark**. Moving only a few outputs does not make a system fast if it must still scan, transfer support across, home or rewrite all others.

**Build an evidence-based, gameplay-valid D&D workload suite before declaring performance compliance.** Sample varied real D&D maps and representative transitions, but first annotate *why* each feature needs physical 3D terrain and where visuals or removable props are better. Retain credible alternative encodings when they change the played experience. For each playable variant, count raised/changed cells, levels, meaningful wall/cliff lengths and connectivity, clustering versus scattered changes, visited rows/banks, and unaffected miniature-bearing regions. Include initial setup, map-to-map and incremental fog-of-war reveals, and what a player sees during the change. A single annotator's interpretation is not a measured gameplay norm: obtain independent/owner feedback for ambiguous cases and label assumed sparse/medium/dense cases honestly. Few useful obstacles can still visit many rows; conversely lifting nearly all ground to depict a water depression may reflect the artist-to-height convention rather than a desired physical battlefield.

Report separately:
- **Gameplay benchmark (<30 s target):** complete end-to-end latency for the representative D&D workload suite, with stated coverage and statistical summaries, plus failures/retries. A representative benchmark definition and qualifying tail metric are still to be established empirically; a favorable hand-picked example is not enough.
- **Dense arbitrary-height stress benchmark:** full/near-full-pin changes, adversarial patterns and maximum achievable update time/throughput. **This is reported and optimized but is no longer, by itself, an unconditional <30 s product rejection gate.** The previous >213 finished-cells/s serial bound assumes all 6,400 need independent work and remains only a dense-case bound, before overhead.
- **Local gameplay updates:** unchanged areas remain supported without deliberately resetting/homing/lifting the full board; avoid disturbing miniatures outside the changed region, quantify non-target movement, and aim for <30 s with time ideally scaling with real changed regions. No tighter local-time or disturbance number is yet established.

Coarse bank/segment/mask-strip updates may be accepted only with quantified effects on unchanged geometry, miniatures, fog-of-war and reliability, plus an explicit product decision. Dense changes still matter for robustness and completeness: distinguish slower-but-functional from unreachable states and from unsafe state transitions.

These rules revise the **workload definition** underlying earlier dense full-map timing gates, not the underlying measurements, models or rejection scopes in existing research. Do not retrospectively rewrite historical experiment results.

**Fog-of-war reveal and wall-oriented updates:** D&D game masters commonly reveal new rooms, passages and barriers as players explore. Sander hypothesizes that maps often contain long, largely horizontal/vertical walls on a square grid; this orientation/clustering is **not yet measured**. Include line segments (horizontal/vertical/diagonal), doors, scattered obstacles, contiguous rooms and incremental reveal sequences in the representative workload corpus. Record changed cells **and which rows, columns and actuator regions** must be visited; sparse walls can still cause slow scanning, repositioning or shared support transfer. Assess whether a movable rotary head, two orthogonal writers or several smaller parallel heads exploit real clustering.

For a known forthcoming reveal, a head may be **pre-positioned** near the next wall/room if that does not move/reveal terrain early, disturb miniatures, or compromise other updates. Report both (1) player-visible reveal latency and (2) complete sustained cycle/preparation/positioning cost. Pre-positioning is a gameplay optimization, not free throughput: show how it behaves when the next reveal is unknown or changes abruptly. Compare typical and adverse sequences; do not optimize against an invented perfect-foreknowledge map.
